# The Problem I Couldn't Fix

*Sometimes, the most meaningful recognition comes when you haven't solved anything at all.*

It's Friday the 13th. Microsoft is having a bad day, which means an awful lot of IT professionals are having an even worse one.

Outlook isn't working. Emails aren't flowing. People are understandably frustrated, and the questions are coming in.

"Is it working yet?"

"Any idea when it'll be fixed?"

"Can you do anything?"

And the answer, frustratingly, is no.

Not because we haven't tried. Not because we don't know what we're doing. But because sometimes the problem sits somewhere entirely outside our control.

You can troubleshoot a faulty laptop, investigate a network issue, restart a service or work through a particularly stubborn technical problem.

But when Microsoft itself is having a wobble, there's only so much you can do.

And telling people that isn't always easy.

## A small moment in a difficult morning

In the middle of the disruption, one of our engineers got in touch about his emails.

I explained that I couldn't fix them. The issue was with Microsoft, and we were essentially waiting for them to resolve it.

His response?

**"I have faith in you, you can fix it."**

Accompanied by a laughing emoji, naturally.

We had a laugh about it.

Then, a couple of minutes later, another message appeared.

**"You're doing a great job, just not enough hours in the day."**

And that one made me stop.

Not for long. There were still things to do, problems to investigate and people waiting for answers.

But long enough to appreciate what had just happened.

Here was someone who needed help. Someone whose working day was being disrupted by a problem I couldn't resolve.

And instead of expressing frustration, he took a moment to offer encouragement.

Not because I'd fixed anything.

Not because I'd performed some technical miracle.

Just because he'd noticed.

## The invisible side of IT

There's an interesting contradiction in working with technology.

When everything works, nobody particularly notices.

Systems are available. Emails arrive. Reports refresh. Applications open. Data flows between platforms. The business carries on.

That's exactly how it should be.

But behind that apparent simplicity is an enormous amount of work.

The maintenance, the monitoring, the development, the troubleshooting, the integrations, the improvements and the countless little interventions that prevent minor issues from becoming major ones.

And then there's the work that never quite makes it onto the project plan.

The quick questions. The unexpected problems. The requests that start with "Sorry, can I just ask you something?"

The things that take five minutes, except they rarely take five minutes.

Much of that work is invisible.

Until something stops working.

Then suddenly, everyone remembers IT exists.

It's not a complaint. It's simply the nature of the job.

And, if we're being fair, most of us wouldn't have chosen this profession if we didn't enjoy solving problems.

But there's a difference between enjoying responsibility and feeling responsible for absolutely everything.

## We're not measured solely by what we fix

I think people working in technical roles can fall into a particular trap.

We measure our own effectiveness by the problems we've solved.

The system we restored. The process we automated. The report we delivered. The issue we finally tracked down after hours of investigation.

And when something can't be fixed, particularly when someone is waiting on us, it can feel like we've somehow fallen short.

Even when the circumstances are completely outside our control.

Of course, intellectually, we know that's nonsense.

I cannot personally repair Microsoft's infrastructure.

Much as I'd occasionally like to think otherwise.

But knowing something logically and feeling it in the moment are two different things.

Which is why that message meant more than the sender probably realised.

He wasn't judging my contribution by whether I could fix his emails.

He was recognising the work that happens the rest of the time.

The effort. The availability. The willingness to help.

And perhaps, most importantly, the fact that there's a person behind the technology.

## The power of an ordinary sentence

We talk a lot about recognition in the workplace.

Performance reviews. Appraisals. Objectives. Awards. Employee engagement initiatives.

All of them have their place.

But I wonder whether we sometimes overlook the simplest form of recognition.

One colleague telling another that they're doing a good job.

Not as part of a formal process.

Not because somebody has asked for feedback.

Not because a project has just been successfully delivered.

But simply because they mean it.

It takes seconds.

And yet, on the right day, those few seconds can make an extraordinary difference.

The person sending the message might forget about it before lunchtime.

The person receiving it might remember it for years.

## Not enough hours in the day

The second half of that message was particularly apt.

"Just not enough hours in the day."

I suspect that's a sentiment shared by an awful lot of people, regardless of their profession.

There's always another task, another improvement, another problem to solve.

And sometimes we become so focused on everything we haven't managed to do that we forget to recognise everything we have.

It's easy to look at an unfinished to-do list and see a lack of progress.

It's harder to see the dozens of things you've already dealt with that weren't even on the list when you started the morning.

Sometimes it takes somebody else to remind you.

And sometimes that reminder comes from the most unexpected place.

## A different kind of fix

As the working day draws to a close, Microsoft's services are beginning to recover.

Emails are starting to flow again. Systems are gradually returning to normal. And somewhere, I imagine, an enormous number of IT professionals are breathing a collective sigh of relief.

The problem is finally being resolved.

Not by me, of course.

But looking back on the day, it's not the outage I'll remember most.

It's a message from an engineer who had every reason to be frustrated but chose to be kind instead.

He probably thought he was just making a passing comment.

For me, it was a reminder that our value at work isn't determined entirely by the number of problems we solve, the tickets we close or the projects we deliver.

Sometimes people notice the effort even when there's no successful outcome to celebrate.

And sometimes they take the time to tell us.

**I couldn't fix his emails that morning.**

**But with two sentences, he made a difficult day feel considerably easier.**

And I think that's worth remembering.
